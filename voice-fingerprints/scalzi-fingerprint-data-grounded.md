# Voice Fingerprint (Data-Grounded) — John Scalzi

**Author:** John Scalzi
**Source file:** *Questions for a Soldier* (2005, Subterranean Press; Old Man's War universe)
**Sample:** the **complete** narrative — 5,241 words / 342 sentences / ~6,989 est. tokens (27,955 chars, tokens = chars/4). The whole story fits under the 6,000-word / 32,000-char cap, so no truncation was needed. Front matter (title page, copyright, and the in-world "CDF Public Relations file" header) was excluded; the sample runs from `——TRANSCRIPT BEGINS——` to `——END TRANSCRIPT——`.
**Method:** Text extracted with `pdftotext` (poppler, raw mode — cleanest of the three tools tested). All numbers below are computed over this sample. Sentence-level metrics were taken after removing the 29 standalone parenthetical stage cues (e.g. `(laughter)`); word/char totals include them.

> **Critical structural note:** This story is a *mock oral transcript*. It is delivered as the recorded Q&A of Captain John Perry answering villagers' questions, framed as a CDF public-relations document. There is **no third-person narrative prose at all** — every word is spoken. This makes the piece an atypical but revealing Scalzi specimen: it isolates his **spoken-voice engine** (first person, direct address, deadpan) with the usual quotation-mark-and-`said`-tag machinery stripped away. Read the metrics through that lens.

## 1. Sentence rhythm and length

Mean **15.2** words but median only **12**, with a high standard deviation (**11.4**) — the classic Scalzi right-skew: a floor of short, plain sentences plus a long tail of cumulative ones. Nearly a quarter of sentences (**24.6%**) are six words or fewer, and **11.1%** are three words or fewer; there are 20 sentences of ≤2 words and 3 true one-word fragments (`Aparna!`, `Impossible!`). At the other end, **11.7%** of sentences run ≥30 words, topping out at a **64-word** periodic sentence built by rule-of-three accumulation (`...keeping your head down..., or trying to save a friend..., or coming face to face with a creature...`). The engine is **staccato setup, then either a clipped punchline or a long rolling build** — rarely a middle register.

## 2. Vocabulary register

Plain, contemporary, conversational — Anglo-Saxon and general-reader. The most frequent content words are abstractions and in-world nouns: `people` (21), `cdf` (20), `union` (19), `colonial`/`colony`/`colonies` (19/13/11), `planet` (17), plus the deliberately vague `thing`/`things` (15). Concreteness arrives only inside anecdotes (`worm`/`worms` 19, `mouth` 10, `ground` 9 — the "eaten alive" set-piece). Filler is a signature, not noise: **`just` appears 23 times**, with `well` (10), `very` (7), `really` (6), `actually` (5), `maybe` (5) reinforcing the off-the-cuff texture. Profanity is present but mild — only **4 tokens total** (`ugly as hell`, `every damn creature`), calibrated for a family-audience speaker.

## 3. Point of view and narrative distance

First person, maximally close, and **performing for a live audience**. `I` alone occurs **182 times (3.5% of all words)**; all first-person-singular forms total 243. Crucially, this is *oral* first person: second-person `you`/`your` appears **117 times (2.2%)** and communal `we`/`us`/`our` 90 times, so first- and second-person pronouns together account for **8.6%** of the sample. Third-person pronouns (78) trail badly. Narrative distance is zero — there is no narrator between reader and speaker at all; the "distance" is instead *social*, a raconteur working a crowd, softening hard content with hedges and jokes before the audience can flinch.

## 4. Dialogue handling

The most instructive section, because the format inverts the usual expectation. There are **zero straight quotation marks** and **zero `said`/`asked`/`replied` dialogue tags**. Speech is attributed by 57 ALL-CAPS speaker labels (`PERRY:`, `KULKARNI:`, `VILLAGER #3:`) — the transcript conceit replaces punctuation-and-tag machinery wholesale. The only curly quotes (5 spans, 25 words) are *embedded* quotation: a joke Perry retells and scare-quotes on `"grass"`/`"seeding"`. Reporting verbs that do appear (`said` 5, `says` 2, `say` 9) are Perry *narrating within his monologue*, not tagging live dialogue. The functional equivalent of action beats is **29 parenthetical stage cues**, 20 of them `(laughter)` — Scalzi uses audience reaction as the beat that a novel would render as "he grinned." Q&A cadence is fast: short villager questions (`Why are you green?`) trigger long Perry answers.

## 5. Description style

Sparse and instrumental — this speaker does not paint scenery. Physical detail is rationed to anecdotes and almost always arrives as a **homely, comic simile rather than sustained observation**: aliens are `ugly as hell — they look like exploded lizards`; an empty planet is `the universe's biggest front lawn`; an anomaly is `a $20 bill on the street`. The **28 em dashes** are the workhorse of description, used for appositive glosses that define a thing mid-sentence (`—they look like exploded lizards—`) rather than for atmosphere. There is no mood-painting, no weather, no lyricism; concrete nouns spike only when the plot needs a body-horror image (worm, mouth, ground) and recede immediately.

## 6. Pacing and paragraph structure

Bimodal by design. Mean paragraph length is 79.4 words but the **median is only 19.5** — a small number of long monologue blocks (the opening intro runs 223 words) sit among many short conversational snaps. **37.9% of paragraphs are a single sentence** (25 of 66), and the 57-turn Q&A structure keeps handing the floor back and forth, so the reader constantly feels progress and never sits inside one voice for long. Pacing is thus **long-answer / quick-exchange / long-answer**, with the stage-cue laughter functioning as a metronome between beats.

## 7. Emotional register

Deadpan comedy as the default surface, with genuine feeling underneath and never wallowed in. The comic machinery is measurable: 27 question marks drive the Q&A, and 20+ `(laughter)` cues mark jokes landing (`Well, there was the time I was eaten.`). Emotion is delivered by *understatement and hard numbers*, not adjectives — the somber turns land through blunt statistics (`eight out of ten of the people you joined with will have died`) rather than emotive prose. The register modulates: light banter → sober war content → a quiet, plainly-stated hope for peace (`simple isn't the same thing as easy`), then a joke to release the tension. Exclamation marks (9) belong mostly to the *villagers*, not Perry — the protagonist stays level while the crowd reacts.

## 8. Signature quirks

- **Mock-document framing.** The whole story is a bureaucratic artifact (CDF file header, `TRANSCRIPT BEGINS/ENDS`, filed-by credit) — a favorite Scalzi device for making SF exposition feel found rather than delivered.
- **Speaker-label dialogue, no quote marks.** 0 quotation marks; 57 caps labels; `(laughter)`-type cues (29) as beats.
- **`just` as a verbal tic** (23) plus a hedge cloud (`well`, `maybe`, `actually`) that casualizes even heavy material.
- **Em dash over ellipsis, decisively:** 28 em dashes, **0 ellipses** — Scalzi cuts and appends; he does not trail off.
- **Direct second-person address** (`you`/`your` 117) and **call-and-response tics** (`Yes, sir.` / `Yes, ma'am.`).
- **Running gags** as structure (green skin = "I ate too much"; the recurring marriage/retirement bit) that reward the reader across the piece.
- **Rule-of-three cumulative sentence** reserved for the emotional peak (the 64-word war sentence), against a baseline of clipped declaratives.

## Skill-ready directives

1. **Write in close, oral first person.** Keep `I` density high (~3–4% of words) and address the reader/audience directly with `you` — aim for first+second person around 8–9% of all words.
2. **Run a bimodal sentence rhythm.** Target a ~12-word median with a ~15-word mean; make ~25% of sentences ≤6 words and let ~10–12% exceed 30, built by rule-of-three accumulation. Cap short.
3. **Default to deadpan.** Deliver emotion and stakes through understatement and hard numbers (`eight out of ten will have died`), not adjectives; keep the narrator level while side characters react.
4. **Punctuate with em dashes, never ellipses.** Use the em dash for appositive glosses and punchlines (`—they look like exploded lizards—`); avoid trailing `...`.
5. **Describe only via homely comic simile.** No mood-painting or weather. Reach for the everyday comparison (`the universe's biggest front lawn`) and drop concrete nouns only inside anecdotes.
6. **Keep filler deliberate.** Salt casual lines with `just`, `well`, `really`, `actually`, `maybe` to sound off-the-cuff — but strip the key/emotional line clean.
7. **Handle dialogue lean.** Prefer `said`/`asked` when tags are needed; skip fancy tag verbs (`replied` etc.); substitute action beats for tags. In transcript/document conceits, use speaker labels and reaction cues instead of quotation marks.
8. **Structure as exchange.** Alternate long explanatory blocks with short single-sentence beats (aim ~35–40% single-sentence paragraphs); keep the floor moving so the reader always feels progress.
9. **Use a found-document frame when exposition is heavy** (transcript, log, file, report) to make world-building feel discovered rather than lectured.
10. **Keep profanity sparse and comic** (a handful of mild tokens — `damn`, `hell`), timed for release rather than grit.
