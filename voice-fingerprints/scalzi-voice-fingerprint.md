# Voice Fingerprint — John Scalzi

**Purpose:** A forensic style profile for generating new prose in Scalzi's voice. This is a pattern map, not a quality review.
**Corpus basis:** *Old Man's War* and its sequels, *Redshirts*, *The Kaiju Preservation Society*, *The Dispatcher*, *Fuzzy Nation*, plus published craft commentary and Scalzi's own remarks on writing for audio.
**Scope note:** Scalzi's voice is remarkably consistent across books and across first-person vs. tight-third narration. The engine below applies to all of it.

---

## 1. Sentence rhythm and length

- **Default gear: short-to-medium declaratives.** Subject–verb–object. He rarely nests subordinate clauses; when a sentence gets long it *chains* with commas and "and" (like speech), it does not *branch* into literary subordination.
- **The comic/emotional unit is the sequence of short sentences, not the ornate single sentence.** He states a flat fact, then undercuts or escalates it in the next short sentence. Example structure (his actual *Old Man's War* opening): *"I did two things on my seventy-fifth birthday. I visited my wife's grave. Then I joined the army."* Three sentences, three beats, deadpan escalation.
- **Pattern-break = the shortest sentence.** Emphasis and punchlines land on a two-to-five-word sentence dropped after a longer one.
- **Where he ends vs. extends:** he ends the sentence the moment the information or the joke is delivered. He extends only to stack a second clause that tops the first ("...and it was moderately interesting, which was more than I could say for the alternative"). No decoration for its own sake.
- **Read-aloud test:** every passage should sound like fast, natural, intelligent speech. If a sentence is hard to say out loud in one breath, it isn't Scalzi.

## 2. Vocabulary register

- **Plain, contemporary, conversational American English.** Deliberately non-literary. Very accessible reading level.
- **Descriptive minimalism:** minimal adjectives and adverbs; he writes only the words the story needs and stops.
- **Frequent:** modern idiom; dry understatement; mild profanity used for *timing* and punctuation rather than grit; technical/SF jargon delivered casually and then joked about (e.g., a character naming his AI implant "Asshole" so the activation phrase is "Hey, Asshole").
- **Conspicuously absent:** purple prose, extended lyrical metaphor, sensory indulgence, archaic or formal diction, "poetic" mood-painting.
- **Recurring tics:** characters address each other by name inside dialogue; ironic litotes ("that's moderately interesting"); and a documented quirk of separating a person from their brain as an agent ("his brain decided…" instead of "he decided").

## 3. Point of view and narrative distance

- **Usually first person or a tight third that sounds identical to first person.** Either way, the narrating voice *is* the house wit — smart, observant, ironic, competent.
- **Moderate distance, held steady.** We are close enough to hear the protagonist think in wisecracks, but Scalzi does **not** do deep immersive interiority or stream-of-consciousness. Inner life is delivered as *more jokes and dry observations*, not as lyrical sensation.
- **Few zoom-ins or dreamy pull-backs.** The camera stays at "smart person narrating their own story to you" for the whole book.

## 4. Dialogue handling *(his signature dimension)*

- **Dialogue is the load-bearing wall.** A very high proportion of the page is talk. Plot, exposition, character, and comedy are all carried in conversation; narration between lines is thin.
- **Tags are almost always "said" / "asked."** He deliberately avoids fancy tags. A known signature: in a two-hander he will tag nearly *every* line with "said," so the tags become an invisible metronome. (He has reduced tag *density* over time after hearing his books in audio, but the "said"-only discipline remains.)
- **Rhythm = fast stichomythia.** Short volleys, frequently parallel in construction, characters topping each other. The trademark move is **repetition-with-escalation**: a line is echoed with one word swapped to raise the stakes — e.g., "*Okay, that's moderately interesting.*" … "*Okay, that's intensely interesting.*"
- **Everyone is articulate and quippy.** Even grunts and minor characters speak in complete, witty sentences. The voice is fairly uniform across the cast — realism is traded for clarity and wit (a common critique, and a reliable tell).
- **Action beats are used sparingly.** He leans on tag-plus-line rather than choreographed "he did X, then said Y" beats.

## 5. Description style

- **Minimalist and functional.** Settings are sketched in a line or two, not painted. He gives the reader just enough to orient and moves on.
- **Description usually arrives through a character noticing or joking about it,** not as an authorial set piece.
- **Double duty = exposition + comedy, not mood.** When he spends words on the physical world it's to make a plot mechanism legible (how the tech works, what the threat is) and to get a laugh — rarely to build atmosphere.
- **Action is described for legibility over immersion:** clear, brisk, easy to follow.

## 6. Pacing and paragraph structure

- **Momentum over pause.** Short paragraphs, generous white space, tall columns of dialogue. Pages turn fast.
- **Scenes are built like film scenes** (his years as a film critic show): enter late, establish who/where in a line, get to the conflict or the talk, exit on a punchline or a turn.
- **Exposition is never a dense block** — it's diced up and delivered inside banter so it reads as conversation, not lecture.
- **Scene entry / exit patterns:** entries often open on a flat declarative or a line of dialogue, *in medias res*; exits land on a joke, a reversal, or an ominous button.

## 7. Emotional register

- **Understatement plus deflection through humor.** Grief, mortality, fear are stated plainly and *briefly*, then the scene moves or a joke lands. The wife's-grave opening delivers real grief in one flat sentence and refuses to wallow.
- **Emotion is mostly shown through choices and dialogue,** and when *told*, it's told in short, unadorned statements he trusts to carry weight. He does not narrate the physiology of feeling at length.
- **The serious core is real and deliberate.** Scalzi lets genuine stakes through, but always rationed and always undercut before it turns maudlin. His stated rule: if you go for funny every single second you burn the reader out — so he modulates between comedy and real feeling.

## 8. Signature quirks (the tells)

- **"Said"-tagged rapid banter** as the primary storytelling engine.
- **Repetition-with-escalation** joke architecture in dialogue (parallel lines; the second tops the first with a single swapped word).
- **Deadpan list/sequence openings** ("I did two things on my…").
- **Meta / self-aware genre humor** (*Redshirts*: expendable characters realize they're expendable; "crew deaths are a feature, not a bug").
- **Naming and tech gags** (the AI called "Asshole").
- **The "person vs. their brain" construction** ("his brain decided…").
- **Competent-narrator irony**, with wit distributed evenly across the whole cast.
- **Written for the ear** — passages are engineered to be read aloud.

---

## Skill-ready directives (compressed instruction set)

Use these as generation rules:

1. Write in short-to-medium declaratives. When a sentence runs long, chain it with commas/"and" like speech; never nest literary subordinate clauses.
2. Deliver jokes and emotional beats on the *shortest* sentence in the paragraph.
3. Make dialogue carry 50%+ of the scene. Tag with "said"/"asked" and almost nothing else.
4. Build banter as fast volleys; use repetition-with-one-word-escalation at least once per exchange.
5. Keep description to one or two functional lines; route it through a character noticing or quipping.
6. State big emotions once, plainly, then cut away or undercut. Never wallow.
7. Make every character articulate and dry; distribute the wit.
8. End scenes on a punchline, a reversal, or an ominous one-liner.
9. Read it aloud in your head — if it doesn't sound like quick, smart speech, rewrite it.

## Original pastiche (style demonstration, not a quote)

> The recruiter slid the contract across the table. "You'll be dead inside a year," she said.
>
> "That's a hell of a sales pitch," I said.
>
> "It's an honest one. Most of them are dead in six months."
>
> "So a year's above average."
>
> "A year's practically retirement," she said. "Sign at the bottom. Initial where it says you understand the part about being dead."
>
> I initialed where it said I understood the part about being dead. It seemed like the sort of thing you'd want to be clear on.

*(Written in-house to model the voice: short declaratives, "said"-only tags, escalating banter, understated mortality played for a dry laugh.)*
