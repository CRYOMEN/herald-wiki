# Voice Fingerprint — Andy Weir

**Purpose:** A forensic style profile for generating new prose in Weir's voice. Pattern map, not quality review.
**Corpus basis:** *The Martian* (Mark Watney) and *Project Hail Mary* (Ryland Grace), plus published craft analysis of *The Martian*'s first chapter and Weir's own comments. *Artemis* (Jazz Bashara) shares the wisecracking first-person engine.
**Scope note:** Weir's signature is the **first-person wisecracking-competent-nerd** solving science problems out loud. *The Martian* uses an epistolary log frame; *Project Hail Mary* uses recovering-memory narration plus a buddy dynamic with an alien. Both run on the same engine.

---

## 1. Sentence rhythm and length

- **Short, punchy, conversational, with frequent fragments for effect.** He builds a plain explanatory sentence, then caps it with a very short reaction or punchline. Actual *Martian* opening: *"I'm pretty much fucked. / That's my considered opinion."*
- **Tight/loose alternation is the defining rhythm.** Personality lines are loose and cluttered on purpose; the *important* lines are stripped bare. Weir deliberately sprinkles filler — "just," "really," "very," "probably," "kind of" — into the casual sentences so the narration feels authentically off-the-cuff, **but keeps the key line clean** ("I'll suffocate," not "I'll probably suffocate"; "I'm in command," short and flat).
- **Pattern-break = one- or two-word sentences and parentheticals** dropped in for comedy ("(yay!)", "(boo!)").
- **Chapter closers are stripped to maximum impact** ("So yeah. I'm fucked.") and often circle back to the chapter's opening line.

## 2. Vocabulary register

- **Plain, contemporary, casual, nerdy.** General-reader accessible. Profanity used freely for comic timing in *The Martian* (dialed back in *Project Hail Mary*).
- **Technical/scientific vocabulary is central — and always immediately translated into plain-language consequences.** He never lets jargon sit unexplained: "a lot of torque" is glossed as "a weak seal"; "airflow and low pressure" becomes "gunk that sealed the gaps." If the reader gets lost in the mechanism, they can skip to the *result* and keep moving.
- **Metaphors are homely and functional, often comic:** the dish "acted like a parachute," the antenna went through the suit "like a bullet through butter," survival by "blood gunk."
- **Conspicuously absent:** literary lyricism, mood-painting, ornate figurative language.
- **Recurring tics:** exclamations; direct address to an imagined reader ("Guys, if you're reading this…"); and **numbers/measurements dropped as characterization** (exact wind speeds, sols, percentages) — precise figures signal a working scientist and back the plot.

## 3. Point of view and narrative distance

- **First person, very close, and performing for an audience.** In *The Martian* the log/journal frame is explicit; in *Project Hail Mary* it's recovering-memory narration. Either way the narrator's personality is *on* every line.
- **The epistolary frame buys retrospective flexibility** close to omniscient: he can compress time, withhold information to build curiosity, and address the reader directly. When a moment needs immediacy, he drops out of "recounting" mode into blow-by-blow, present-feeling scene (e.g., the injury sequence).
- **Emotionally light distance:** we're inside his head, but his head is mostly running jokes and problem-solving, not deep feeling.

## 4. Dialogue handling

- **Varies by book, same DNA.** *The Martian*'s Mars sections are near-solo — "dialogue" is Watney talking to his log; the Earth/NASA sections add snappy institutional banter (rendered in third person). *Project Hail Mary* is dialogue-central: Grace and the alien "Rocky" are a two-hander buddy comedy.
- **Tags are simple; banter is quick, dry, and competent.** Institutional characters trade wisecracks while doing their jobs.
- **Exposition-as-conversation:** characters (and the narrator to himself) *explain the science to each other*, turning info-dumps into dialogue.
- **The alien-dialogue register (Rocky) is a signature:** a stripped, literal, limited-vocabulary way of speaking ("Question." / plain declaratives) that becomes a running source of warmth and comedy.

## 5. Description style

- **Functional and science-forward.** Physical description exists to make a technical problem *legible* and to set up its solution — concrete, measurable, frequently numeric.
- **Sensory detail is deployed selectively at high-tension peaks** (ears "popping painfully," antenna "like a bullet through butter"), then he pulls back to procedure. Description routinely **does double duty as plot mechanism** — the broken comms dish that's described is *also* the reason he can't call home.
- **Almost no atmospheric/mood description.** The environment is a set of engineering constraints, not a landscape.

## 6. Pacing and paragraph structure

- **Momentum with deliberate, frequent stopping points.** Section breaks land every few pages so the reader constantly feels progress. Explanations are broken into digestible, sequential steps.
- **The metronome loop:** (a) tense procedural problem → (b) plain-language explanation → (c) comic release → (d) new problem. Repeat. This four-beat cycle is the structural signature.
- **Short paragraphs.** Scenes/chapters end on a hook or a punchline, and chapters frequently **come full circle** to their opening line for a hit of closure before the next hook.

## 7. Emotional register

- **Emotion handled through humor and action — almost never wallowing.** Fear or despair is acknowledged in one line and then *converted into a task*: "work the problem." Optimism-under-pressure is the default setting.
- **When emotion is told, it's brief and immediately undercut:** a "deep and profound desire to just fucking die" is followed at once by getting up and solving the next thing. Sincerity (the "it wasn't your fault, you did what you had to do" note to the crew) is stated plainly, then he moves on.
- **Likability is deliberately engineered:** self-deprecation, refusing to blame others, cracking jokes mid-disaster. The register is "competent underdog keeping his chin up."

## 8. Signature quirks (the tells)

- **Log / journal entry headers** ("LOG ENTRY: SOL 6").
- **Open a chapter with a bang, close by echoing the opening** (full-circle structure).
- **Parenthetical asides for jokes and reactions** ("(yay!)", "(boo!)", "(and a hole in me, of course)").
- **Intentional filler words** for authenticity in casual lines, contrasted against stripped-clean key lines.
- **Jargon → plain implication** on every technical fact ("so what this means is…").
- **Numbers and measurements as characterization.**
- **Direct address to an imagined future reader.**
- **Understatement-by-contrast humor:** a softening qualifier bolted to a catastrophic noun ("I'll *just kind of* explode").
- **The "work the problem" structure:** panic → joke → method → result.

---

## Skill-ready directives (compressed instruction set)

1. Narrate first person, chatty, performing for an imagined reader (a log, a report, a recovered memory).
2. Alternate loose cluttered personality lines ("just," "really," "kind of") with stripped-clean key lines. Never clutter the important sentence.
3. Center a concrete technical problem. Explain the science in plain words, then always state the plain-language *consequence*.
4. Run the four-beat loop: problem → explanation → joke → new problem. Break sections often so the reader feels progress.
5. Use homely, functional, slightly comic metaphors ("like a bullet through butter"). No lyricism.
6. Drop exact numbers to characterize a competent narrator and to back the plot.
7. Handle fear with a one-line acknowledgment, then convert it into a task. Optimism under pressure.
8. Use parentheticals for jokes; end chapters on a punchline that echoes the opening line.

## Original pastiche (style demonstration, not a quote)

> LOG ENTRY: SOL 41
>
> Okay, so the water reclaimer is dead. Really, thoroughly dead. I did the diagnostic twice because the first result was so bad I assumed I'd screwed it up.
>
> Here's the problem: without the reclaimer I lose about 400 milliliters of water a day to breathing and sweat. I have 62 liters. You can do that math, but I already did it, because I'm the one who's going to die of it. That's 155 days. Sounds like a lot! It is not a lot.
>
> But here's the thing nobody tells you about certain death: it's basically just a plumbing problem with a deadline. And I am *great* at plumbing problems.
>
> So let's get to work.

*(Written in-house to model the voice: log header, cluttered casual lines vs. clean key lines, jargon glossed into consequence, numbers as character, panic converted straight into "let's get to work.")*
